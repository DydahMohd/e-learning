Module 1 of 5

Foundations of Population & Demographic Statistics

Core demographic concepts, population change, data sources and quality.

Demographic statistics describe the size, composition, distribution and change of human populations. They support planning for education, health, labour markets, housing, social protection and regional development across the East African Community.

In this module you will:

1
Define population, demography and demographic statistics.
2
Explain the demographic balancing equation.
3
Distinguish stocks, flows, rates, ratios and proportions.
4
Identify major demographic data sources.
5
Apply basic data-quality principles.

✅ By the end you will be able to:

  • Explain how births, deaths and migration change a population.
  • Select appropriate sources for common demographic indicators.
  • Recognise common quality problems such as undercoverage, age misstatement and delayed registration.
2

What is demography?

Learning slide

Demography is the statistical study of human populations. It examines how many people there are, who they are, where they live and how populations change over time.

Population size
The number of people in a defined geographic area at a specified reference time.
Population structure
Composition by age, sex and other characteristics.
Population distribution
How people are spatially distributed across regions, districts, urban and rural areas.
Population dynamics
Change caused by fertility, mortality and migration.

Why demographic statistics matter

Population statistics provide denominators for many indicators. For example, health, education and labour statistics often require reliable population estimates by age and sex.

3

The demographic balancing equation

Learning slide

The population at the end of a period is determined by the opening population plus births and immigration, less deaths and emigration.

P₂ = P₁ + B − D + I − EP₁ = opening population; B = births; D = deaths; I = immigrants; E = emigrants; P₂ = closing population

🧮 Hypothetical EAC example

A district begins the year with 800,000 people. During the year there are 24,000 births, 6,000 deaths, 9,000 immigrants and 5,000 emigrants.

800,000 + 24,000 − 6,000 + 9,000 − 5,000 = 822,000
The district ends the year with an estimated population of 822,000.
Knowledge check: Which set contains the direct components of population change?
4

Stocks, flows, ratios and rates

Learning slide
MeasureMeaningExample
StockA quantity measured at a point in time.Population on census night.
FlowEvents occurring during a period.Births registered during a year.
ProportionA part divided by the whole; numerator is included in denominator.Share of population aged 0–14.
RatioOne quantity divided by another distinct quantity.Sex ratio: males per 100 females.
RateFrequency of events relative to population exposed to the event, usually over time.Crude birth rate per 1,000 population.

Correct denominator matters

A demographic rate should use the population actually exposed to the event whenever possible. Age-specific rates therefore provide more analytical detail than crude rates.

5

Major demographic data sources

Learning slide
🏠 Population & housing census
Near-complete enumeration at a specified reference time; provides small-area population structure and distribution.
🧾 CRVS
Civil registration and vital statistics record births, deaths and other vital events continuously.
📋 Household surveys
Sample-based sources that can collect fertility, mortality, migration and socioeconomic characteristics.
🏛️ Administrative systems
School, health, immigration, social security and other operational records can complement demographic sources.
🪪 Population registers
Continuous systems that maintain information on resident populations where such registers exist.
NeedOften suitable source
Small-area population countCensus
Continuous births and deathsCRVS
Detailed fertility historyHousehold survey
Border movementsImmigration/administrative records, complemented by surveys or census
6

Data quality and metadata

Learning slide

High-quality demographic statistics require transparent concepts, consistent classifications and well-documented methods.

Quality dimensionQuestions to ask
CoverageWere all intended people or events captured? Is there undercount or duplication?
AccuracyAre ages, dates, sex and locations correctly reported and coded?
TimelinessHow quickly are results available after the reference period?
CoherenceAre census, survey, CRVS and administrative estimates reasonably consistent?
ComparabilityAre concepts and methods stable across places and time?
AccessibilityCan users find data, metadata and explanations?

Common demographic data issues

Age heaping, under-registration of births or deaths, household omissions, double counting, misclassification of residence and inconsistent geographic boundaries can distort indicators.

2

Age and sex structure

Learning slide

Age and sex are two of the most important demographic variables. Standard age groups often include single years, five-year groups, or broad functional groups such as 0–14, 15–64 and 65+.

Sex Ratio = (Number of males ÷ Number of females) × 100Often expressed as males per 100 females.

🧮 Example

If a population has 510,000 males and 500,000 females:

(510,000 ÷ 500,000) × 100 = 102
There are 102 males for every 100 females.
3

Dependency ratios

Learning slide

A dependency ratio compares populations commonly considered economically dependent with the working-age population. It is a structural indicator, not a direct measure of actual employment or dependency.

Total Dependency Ratio = ((P₀₋₁₄ + P₆₅₊) ÷ P₁₅₋₆₄) × 100
Child dependency ratio
Population aged 0–14 relative to working-age population.
Old-age dependency ratio
Population aged 65+ relative to working-age population.

🧮 Example

A population has 420,000 people aged 0–14, 530,000 aged 15–64 and 50,000 aged 65+.

((420,000 + 50,000) ÷ 530,000) × 100 ≈ 88.7
There are about 89 people in the dependent-age groups per 100 people aged 15–64.
4

Population pyramids

Learning slide

A population pyramid plots age groups vertically and population counts or percentages horizontally, typically showing males and females on opposite sides.

Expansive shape
Broad base and narrower upper ages; often associated with relatively high fertility and a young population.
More stationary shape
Similar widths across many age groups; suggests slower change in age structure.
Constrictive shape
Narrower younger cohorts relative to middle ages; often associated with lower fertility and population ageing.

Look for irregularities

Bulges, indentations and sex imbalances may reflect past fertility changes, mortality shocks, migration, conflict, boundary changes or data-quality problems.

Knowledge check: What does a very broad base on a population pyramid usually suggest?
5

Population density and distribution

Learning slide
Population Density = Population ÷ Land Area (km²)

Density is useful for comparing settlement concentration, but national or regional averages can hide major local differences.

🧮 Hypothetical example

A region has 2,400,000 residents and a land area of 30,000 km².

2,400,000 ÷ 30,000 = 80 persons/km²

Distribution is more than density

Maps, urban-rural classifications and subnational tabulations help identify where people are concentrated and how access to services varies spatially.

6

Population growth and the demographic dividend

Learning slide
Growth Rate (%) ≈ ((P₂ − P₁) ÷ P₁) × 100For a simple one-period calculation. Compound annual growth is preferable across multiple years.

The demographic dividend refers to potential economic benefits when the working-age share of the population rises relative to dependent ages. It is not automatic: outcomes depend on health, education, skills, employment, productivity and institutions.

Interpret carefully

A lower dependency ratio does not itself guarantee higher economic growth. It creates a potential opportunity that requires supportive policies and labour-market absorption.

Knowledge check: Which statement about the demographic dividend is correct?

2

Fertility concepts

Learning slide
Fertility
Actual childbearing performance, commonly measured through live births.
Fecundity
Biological capacity to reproduce. Fecundity and observed fertility are not the same.

Fertility is influenced by demographic, social, economic, cultural and behavioural factors, including age at union, contraceptive use, breastfeeding, education and desired family size.

3

Crude birth rate and general fertility rate

Learning slide
CBR = (Live Births ÷ Mid-year Population) × 1,000
GFR = (Live Births ÷ Women aged 15–49) × 1,000

🧮 Example

A population of 1,000,000 records 28,000 live births. There are 250,000 women aged 15–49.

  • CBR = 28,000 ÷ 1,000,000 × 1,000 = 28 per 1,000 population.
  • GFR = 28,000 ÷ 250,000 × 1,000 = 112 per 1,000 women aged 15–49.

Why “crude”?

The crude birth rate uses the total population in its denominator, including people not exposed to childbearing. It is easy to compute but strongly affected by age-sex structure.

4

Age-specific fertility rates (ASFR)

Learning slide
ASFRₓ = (Births to women in age group x ÷ Women in age group x) × 1,000

ASFRs show the age pattern of childbearing and allow comparisons that are less distorted by differences in age structure.

Age groupInterpretation
15–19Adolescent fertility.
20–24 to 30–34Often contain the largest concentration of births, depending on the population.
35–49Later-age fertility.
Knowledge check: Which denominator is used for an ASFR for women aged 20–24?
5

Total fertility rate (TFR)

Learning slide

The total fertility rate summarises age-specific fertility rates into the average number of children a woman would have if she experienced the current age-specific fertility schedule throughout her reproductive years, subject to the assumptions of the period measure.

TFR = 5 × ΣASFR ÷ 1,000When ASFRs are expressed per 1,000 women in five-year age groups.

TFR is a synthetic cohort measure

It describes the fertility schedule of a particular period. It is not the observed completed family size of an actual group of women unless the schedule remains unchanged.

6

Other reproductive indicators

Learning slide
Gross reproduction rate
Average number of daughters a woman would bear under current fertility rates, ignoring female mortality.
Net reproduction rate
Average number of daughters a newborn girl would bear over her lifetime under current fertility and female mortality schedules.
Mean age at childbearing
Average age of mothers at childbirth, weighted by age-specific fertility.

Replacement-level fertility

Replacement level refers to the fertility required for one generation to replace itself in the long run in the absence of migration. The exact level depends on mortality conditions and the sex ratio at birth; it should not be treated as a universal fixed constant for every population.

3

4

5
Explain life expectancy.
2

Crude and specific death rates

Learning slide
CDR = (Deaths during year ÷ Mid-year Population) × 1,000
Age-specific Death Rate = (Deaths at age x ÷ Population at age x) × k

🧮 Example

If 7,500 deaths occur in a mid-year population of 1,250,000:

(7,500 ÷ 1,250,000) × 1,000 = 6.0 deaths per 1,000 population

Crude rates can mislead comparisons

Two populations can have different crude death rates simply because one population is older. Age-standardized rates are often preferable for comparing mortality levels.

3

Infant and child mortality

Learning slide
IMR = (Deaths under age 1 ÷ Live births) × 1,000
Neonatal mortality
Deaths in the first 28 completed days of life relative to live births.
Post-neonatal mortality
Deaths after the neonatal period but before age one.
Under-five mortality
Probability of dying before age five, usually expressed per 1,000 live births.

Rate vs probability

Some child mortality indicators are formally probabilities derived from life-table or survey methods, even though they are often informally called “rates.”

4

Mortality standardization

Learning slide

Standardization removes the effect of differing age structures so mortality levels can be compared more fairly.

Direct standardization
Applies observed age-specific rates to a common standard population.
Indirect standardization
Applies standard age-specific rates to the study population; often summarized using a standardized mortality ratio.
Knowledge check: Why is age standardization useful when comparing mortality?
5

Life tables

Learning slide

A life table describes the mortality experience of a hypothetical cohort subjected to a set of age-specific mortality rates.

SymbolMeaning
lₓNumber of survivors to exact age x from the life-table radix.
dₓDeaths between exact ages x and x+n.
qₓProbability of dying between x and x+n.
LₓPerson-years lived between x and x+n.
TₓTotal person-years remaining after age x.
eₓExpectation of life at age x.
6

Life expectancy

Learning slide
Life expectancy at birth
The average number of years a newborn would live if the current age-specific mortality rates remained in effect throughout life.

Life expectancy is a period summary of current mortality conditions. It is not a prediction of exactly how long any individual newborn will live.

eₓ = Tₓ ÷ lₓLife-table relationship between remaining person-years and survivors at exact age x.
Knowledge check: What does life expectancy at birth summarize?
7

Mortality data sources and quality

Learning slide
CRVS
Preferred continuous source for deaths and causes of death where registration is complete and medically certified.
Census and surveys
Can estimate adult and child mortality where CRVS is incomplete, using household deaths, birth histories or survival questions.
Health systems
Facility records support cause-specific analysis but may not represent all deaths in the population.

Completeness is critical

If deaths are under-registered, crude and age-specific mortality rates can be biased downward. Analysts should assess completeness before interpretation.

2

Migration concepts

Learning slide
Migration
A change in usual residence from one geographic area to another, defined using a specified spatial boundary and time criterion.
Internal migration
Movement between areas within the same country.
International migration
Movement involving a change of country of usual residence.
In-migration / immigration
Movement into the area being studied.
Out-migration / emigration
Movement out of the area being studied.

Definitions of “usual residence” and duration should be documented because they affect who is classified as a migrant.

3

Migration measures

Learning slide
Net Migration = In-migrants − Out-migrants
Net Migration Rate = (Net Migration ÷ Mid-year Population) × 1,000

🧮 Example

A region records 18,000 in-migrants and 11,000 out-migrants in a population of 700,000.

  • Net migration = 18,000 − 11,000 = 7,000.
  • Net migration rate = 7,000 ÷ 700,000 × 1,000 = 10 per 1,000.
Knowledge check: If immigration is 30,000 and emigration is 38,000, net migration equals:
4

Urbanization

Learning slide
Urbanization
Increase over time in the proportion of a population living in areas classified as urban.
Urban Share (%) = (Urban Population ÷ Total Population) × 100

Urban population growth can result from natural increase within urban areas, rural-to-urban migration, international migration, and reclassification or expansion of urban boundaries.

Comparability issue

Urban definitions differ across countries and can change over time. Regional comparisons should always review national definitions and boundary changes.

5

Population estimates vs projections

Learning slide
TermMeaning
Population estimateAn assessment of population size or structure for a past or current date, using available evidence.
Population projectionA conditional calculation of future population based on specified assumptions about fertility, mortality and migration.
ForecastA projection presented as the analyst's judgement of a likely future path.

Projections are conditional

A projection is not a certainty. Its results depend on the base population, model and assumptions.

6

The cohort-component method

Learning slide

The cohort-component method advances each age-sex cohort over time, applying mortality, adding births and incorporating migration.

1 · Base population
Start with a population by age and sex for a defined date.
2 · Survival
Apply survival probabilities to move cohorts into older ages.
3 · Births
Apply fertility assumptions to women of reproductive age and distribute births by sex.
4 · Migration
Add or subtract migrants by age and sex.
5 · Repeat
Continue for each projection interval.
Future population = Surviving population + Births + Net migrationApplied by age and sex in a cohort-component framework.
7

Projection scenarios and uncertainty

Learning slide

Projection exercises commonly use multiple scenarios to show how alternative assumptions affect results.

Low scenario
A defined combination of assumptions producing relatively lower population growth.
Medium scenario
A central or reference set of assumptions.
High scenario
A defined combination producing relatively higher growth.

Uncertainty grows with horizon

Near-term projections are strongly influenced by the existing age structure. Longer-term results become increasingly sensitive to fertility, mortality and migration assumptions.

Knowledge check: Which method explicitly applies fertility, mortality and migration to age-sex cohorts?
8

Summary & key takeaways

Learning slide

🎯 Course-level takeaways

1
Population change is determined by births, deaths, immigration and emigration.
2
Age-sex structure affects crude demographic rates and determines many planning needs.
3
Fertility is best understood through age-specific measures and summary indicators such as TFR.
4
Mortality analysis uses age-specific rates, child mortality measures and life tables; comparisons often require standardization.
5
Migration and urbanization can reshape populations quickly, while projections show conditional future paths.
6
Good demographic analysis depends on strong census, CRVS, survey and administrative data, supported by clear metadata and quality assessment.
